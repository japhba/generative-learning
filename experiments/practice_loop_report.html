Generative learning · toy model 1 · ring

Practice can repair a map that ordinary training gets wrong

A learner sees only single steps in a small hidden world. Trained the usual way on those steps, it builds an internal map on which long routes fail. If it practises instead, inventing routes, checking them against what it already knows and reinforcing the ones that work, route success rises from {{start}} to {{practice}}. It gets no new data.

Summary

  1. The question. Can a loop of generate → check → learn make a generator better than its own training objective allows, without any new data from the world?
  2. Yes, in this toy. The best possible solution of the generator's training objective is a bad map: it folds the 12 states onto 4 points. Practice moves the generator to a better map. Route success rises while the training objective gets worse, so practice is doing something that more of the original training could not.
  3. The checker's verdicts drive it. Practising without verdicts, or with verdicts shuffled between routes, leaves route success where it started.
  4. Practice is not the only way, though. A different objective on the same single-step data (slow-feature learning) finds the good map directly ({{slow}}). The result shows that practice can beat this particular training objective, not that practice is necessary.

The setup

World and task

Twelve states sit on a hidden ring. Action + moves one step one way round the ring, action − one step the other way. A task gives a start and a goal at least two steps apart, and the goal must be reached within six actions. There are 108 such tasks.

What the learner sees

Five observations of every (state, action) pair, 120 in total, each of the form “in state \(s\), action \(a\) led to state \(s'\)”. Each observation is corrupted with probability 5% (its next state is replaced by a random one). The learner never sees a multi-step route, and gets no further data after these 120.

Checker (recognition model)

For every state and action, it stores the next state seen most often among the five observations, then freezes this table. With it, the checker can play out any proposed sequence of actions and say whether it reaches the goal. In all {{runs}} worlds the table came out exactly right.

Generator (generation model)

It places each state \(i\) at an angle \(\theta_i\) on a circle, \(\mathbf{u}_i=(\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)\). In state \(s\) with goal \(g\) it chooses + with probability

\[p(+\mid s,g)=\sigma\big(6\sin(\theta_g-\theta_s)\big),\]

so it tends to step + when the goal lies anticlockwise of the current state on its internal circle. These 12 angles are all it learns.

Two ways to train the generator

Ordinary training (the baseline)

For each observed single step \(s\to s'\), predict which action was taken. This is the generator's original objective: reconstructing the observed data. Its loss is called the single-step loss below.

Practice

  1. Pick a start and a goal.
  2. The generator proposes two routes, one action at a time. After each action the checker supplies the next state.
  3. The checker says whether each route reached the goal.
  4. If one route succeeded and the other failed, shift the angles towards the choices made on the successful route and away from those on the failed one.

There is no new data and no outside judge. Every route and every verdict comes from the learner's own two parts.

The exact learning rule

While a route runs, each step \(t\) leaves a trace \(e_t = 6\,(a_t-p_t)\cos(\theta_g-\theta_{s_t})\), where \(a_t=1\) if the step was + and \(p_t\) is the probability the generator gave to +. Once both routes of a pair are judged, with \(r\) this route's success and \(b\) its partner's (each 0 or 1):

\[\begin{gathered}\Delta\theta_g=\eta\,(r-b)\sum_t e_t,\\ \Delta\theta_{s_t}=-\eta\,(r-b)\,e_t .\end{gathered}\]

Each update averages 32 pairs (64 routes), with \(\eta=0.1\), for 3,000 updates. Only quantities at the current state and the goal enter, multiplied by a single success signal, so this is a reward-modulated, Hebb-like rule. Nothing is back-propagated through the checker. On average the update equals the gradient of the route success predicted by the checker: it is REINFORCE, with the partner route serving as the baseline.

Why ordinary training gets stuck

Ordinary training is scored one step at a time. A single step is predicted most confidently when neighbouring states sit 90° apart on the generator's circle, because \(\sin 90^\circ = 1\) gives the largest possible logit, 6. Twelve steps of 90° go round the circle three times, so states 1, 5 and 9 land on the same point, as do 2·6·10, 3·7·11 and 4·8·12. This folded map is the exact global optimum of ordinary training (loss {{lossStart}}), yet only {{start}} of routes succeed on it.

A map that goes round once (30° per step) is good for routes ({{ideal}} success) but worse on single steps: \(\sin 30^\circ = \tfrac12\) halves the logit and raises the loss to {{lossIdeal}}. The two objectives genuinely disagree, so no amount of ordinary training reaches the good map.

On the folded map the angle to a goal is a multiple of 90°. Goals 2 or 4 steps ahead give \(\sin = 0\), a coin flip, and a goal 3 steps ahead gives \(\sin 270^\circ = -1\), so the generator is almost certain it should step the wrong way. On the once-round map every goal up to 5 steps ahead points the right way. (A goal 6 steps away is equally far in both directions, so 50% is correct there.)

The same change in terms of the weight matrix

Stack the embeddings into \(\mathbf{U}=[\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_{12}]\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 12}\). The folded and the once-round map have the same singular values, \((\sqrt6,\sqrt6)\), so growth of the weights is not what changes. Their right singular vectors differ: the once-round map spans the slowest sine/cosine pattern around the ring (the first Fourier pair), the folded map the third. Practice raises the overlap with the first pair from 0 to {{mode1}} on average. Projecting the trained weights onto the first pair alone gives {{keepMode1}} route success, and removing that pair leaves {{removeMode1}}. Orientation matters too: mirroring the final map keeps its singular values and subspace but drops success to {{reflect}}.

What this shows, and what it doesn't

Shows

  • A generator can solve its training objective perfectly and still hold the wrong map for chaining steps together.
  • Practice judged by the learner's own checker can repair that map, with no new data and a local, reward-modulated learning rule.
  • The verdicts are essential, and the repair is visible directly in the weights.

Does not show

  • That practice is necessary: slow-feature learning on the same data, or planning with the checker's table, also works.
  • That checking needs fewer examples than generating. Here the checker simply stores a full table, while the generator has to compress it into 12 angles.
  • Anything about one's own versus others' examples (see below).
  • Anything about depth or language models: the generator is one embedding layer with a fixed comparison.
  • That every update helps. The rule follows the gradient of expected success, but single updates can hurt and some runs end low.

How this connects to the project

The project asks whether a model learns more from what it generates itself than from other models' examples. So far, the in-context studies find an own advantage only for applying a demonstrated multi-step procedure (PROC: +5.3 pp). This toy isolates a mechanism that could sit underneath such an effect: practice turns knowledge of single steps into a map that supports multi-step composition.

It does not yet produce an own-versus-foreign difference. Nothing in the model makes the learner's own routes special, since correct routes from elsewhere could be fed through the same rule. A self advantage would need foreign routes that are correct but harder for this particular learner to use, which is the project's current hypothesis.

Theory, briefly

What is guaranteed about improvement

On average the update is \(\eta\) times the gradient of the success that the checker predicts, so following it in the small-step limit (gradient flow) improves that success. When the checker is uncertain, the same holds for success averaged over its belief, which is the Bayes-expected success given the data. That is an average over worlds, which is why some individual worlds get worse.

Single updates are not guaranteed to help. At the exact folded start the gradient is zero by symmetry, and the expected first update slightly lowers success. The randomness of sampled routes breaks the symmetry.

An optional acceptance step gives a guarantee at extra cost. Propose 1,000 updates, test them on 20,000 fresh internal routes, and keep them only if success clearly improves. With high probability (at least 96.8% per world) every accepted block then truly improves success. In {{certWorlds}} worlds it accepted {{certBlocks}} blocks and moved mean success from {{certStart}} to {{certFinal}}.

An in-context version of the same learner

Store each update as a key–value entry (key: state identity, value: its change of angle) and read the entries back with attention. A simulation with this growing memory reproduces the weight-learning run's actions and rewards over all 3,000 updates. This is a constructed equivalence, in the spirit of “attention as fast weights”. It does not show that a trained transformer discovers or runs this circuit.

Related work

Learning from imagined experience with a learned model is Dyna (Sutton, 1990); the rule is REINFORCE with a paired baseline, and has a reward-weighted-regression reading (Dayan & Hinton, 1997). That inverse-dynamics representations can be poor for control is known (Rakelly et al., 2021), and the slow-feature alternative is Laplacian representation learning (Mahadevan & Maggioni, 2007; Wu et al., 2018). What the toy adds is an explicit geometric case in which the optimum of the original objective is provably the wrong map, together with the controls above.
