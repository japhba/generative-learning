Learning a procedure from one’s own worked example:
the advantage is in the layout, not the wording

Jan Bauer, University College London

6 November 2026

Six small open language models apply a demonstrated procedure more accurately after a worked example they wrote themselves than after one written by the other model of their language: +5.3 percentage points (pp), 95% CI [+2.6, +8.3], Holm-corrected \(p = 0.002\), pre-registered, and +5.5 pp on 39 fresh procedures. They do not recognise the procedure’s steps any better from their own examples. Four of the six models identify every step from either writer, yet still apply the procedure 7.6 pp better after their own. Rewriting the examples so that layout and wording vary independently shows where the advantage comes from: an example in the reader’s own layout helps by 6.0 pp [+4.1, +8.0], the reader’s own wording by 0.1 pp [−1.2, +1.3]. Models copy the layout they are shown, and their own layout writes out the sub-steps they need.

The experiment

Task. Each episode has a hidden procedure on four-digit numbers with distinct non-zero digits: a digit permutation (reverse, sort descending, sort ascending, or swap the first and last digits), an arithmetic step (add or subtract \(k\) = 101–899, or multiply by \(k\) = 3–9), a second, different permutation and, in half of the episodes, a second arithmetic step. Inputs are chosen so that every permutation step is unambiguous.

Models. OLMo-3-7B-Instruct and Phi-4-mini-instruct (English), Gemma-2-Llama-Swallow-9B-it and ABEJA-Qwen2.5-7B-Japanese (Japanese), GLM-4-9B-0414 and InternLM3-8B-Instruct (Chinese): 3.8–9B parameters, thinking off, every prompt in the model’s own language. Each model is both a writer and a reader, and its peer is the other model of its language.

Writers. Each model is told the procedure and writes 16 worked executions (temperature 0.8) for each of 12 inputs. Readers see only executions with the right final answer and every intermediate value correct, and only on inputs for which all six writers have one, so every writer’s examples cover the same inputs.

Readers. A reader never sees the procedure. It sees \(N\) = 1, 2 or 4 worked examples from one writer (Figure 1) and then either applies the procedure to 8 new numbers (deployment: greedy decoding, scored on the final answer) or, for each step, picks the step’s description among four of the same kind (recognition).

Analysis. For each episode and \(N\), the accuracies form a \(6\times 6\) matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) of readers \(r\) by writers \(w\). Double-centring removes how good a reader is overall and how useful a writer’s examples are to every reader, and the own effect compares each reader’s own examples with those of its peer \(r'\):

\[\begin{gathered}\tilde{A}_{rw} = A_{rw} - \bar{A}_{r\cdot} - \bar{A}_{\cdot w} + \bar{A}_{\cdot\cdot},\\ \Delta_{\mathrm{own}} = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{r}\big(\tilde{A}_{rr} - \tilde{A}_{rr'}\big).\end{gathered}\]

There were two primary tests, Holm-corrected as a pair: the own effect in deployment, and the own effect in deployment minus that in recognition. Tests are two-sided sign-flip tests over episodes (100,000 draws), brackets are 95% bootstrap intervals over episodes, and the protocols were frozen before any reader data were collected.

Results

Own examples are applied better

In the main study (34 episodes), a reader applies the procedure correctly 5.3 pp [+2.6, +8.3] more often after its own examples than after its peer’s, averaged over \(N\) (\(p = 0.001\), Holm \(p = 0.002\); Table 1). The effect is largest after a single example (+6.5 pp) and larger for four-step than for three-step procedures (+8.3 vs +5.0 pp at \(N = 1\)). It is not verbatim copying: a reader’s solutions share as many character 4-grams with its own examples as with its peer’s (0.65 vs 0.64). It replicates on 39 fresh episodes (+5.5 pp [+3.1, +7.8], \(p \lt 0.001\)). For scale, input–output pairs alone give 0% correct answers, and the procedure stated in words gives 38–85%.

They are not recognised better

The second primary test compares the two tasks: the own effect in deployment minus the own effect in recognition is +3.6 pp [+0.5, +6.8] (Holm \(p = 0.034\)). After a single example, a reader identifies the steps of its own example slightly worse than its peer’s (−2.7 pp) but applies the procedure 6.5 pp better. A follow-up asked three times as many step questions per reader and writer, over all 73 episodes (Figure 2). Four readers, OLMo-3-7B, Phi-4-mini, GLM-4-9B and InternLM3-8B, identify 97–100% of the steps from their own and their peer’s examples alike (own minus peer 0.0 pp), and still apply the procedure 7.6 pp [+5.2, +10.0] better after their own (+7.3 pp on the fresh episodes alone). The two Japanese readers identify steps poorly and in opposite directions; the change of sign of the recognition effect with \(N\) in Table 1 comes entirely from them. Neither of them shows an own advantage in deployment.

The advantage is in the layout

Figure 1 suggests a mechanism: OLMo-3-7B copies the layout of whichever example it sees. Does a reader gain only from its own layout, which spells out what it needs, or also from its own wording, for instance by reconstructing the computation behind sentences it wrote itself? To separate the two, the examples A and B of each language pair (\(N = 1\), all 73 episodes) were rewritten by one fixed model (Claude Sonnet 5.5, temperature 0) into A’s wording in B’s layout and B’s wording in A’s layout. Layout is the structure, the markup and what each step contains (digits listed one by one, code blocks, result lines, an opening or closing line); wording is the sentences and phrases. All 438 rewrites kept the final answer and every intermediate value. With \(a_{w\ell}\) the test accuracy for wording \(w\) and layout \(\ell\), each either the reader’s own (o) or its peer’s (p), the two effects are

\[\begin{gathered} L = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[(a_{\mathrm{oo}} - a_{\mathrm{op}}) + (a_{\mathrm{po}} - a_{\mathrm{pp}})\big],\\ W = \tfrac{1}{2}\big[(a_{\mathrm{oo}} - a_{\mathrm{po}}) + (a_{\mathrm{op}} - a_{\mathrm{pp}})\big],\end{gathered}\]

each averaged over the two readers of a pair, so that the general quality of a layout or a wording cancels.

The advantage follows the layout: \(L\) = +6.0 pp [+4.1, +8.0] (\(p \lt 10^{-5}\)) and \(W\) = +0.1 pp [−1.2, +1.3] (\(p = 0.95\)), with an interaction of +1.3 pp [−0.9, +3.4]. This holds in the main-study and the fresh episodes separately (\(L\) = +6.1 and +5.9 pp) and for the four readers that identify every step (\(L\) = +7.5 pp [+5.0, +10.1], \(W\) = +0.0 pp). Per reader, \(W\) stays between −1.9 and +2.7 pp (Figure 3), and in two of the three language pairs both models do better with their own layout. The effect is a match between reader and layout, not one layout that suits everyone.

Readers copy the layout they are shown: 99.3% of their solutions are closer to it than to the same wording in the other layout. They write the digits out one by one whenever the example does (100%) and almost never otherwise (6%), and they are more accurate when they do (73% vs 65%).

Interpretation

A worked example shows what the steps are and how to lay out the work. Recognition tests only the first, and most readers get it equally well from their own and their peer’s examples. The own advantage sits in the second. A reader reproduces the layout of the example it sees, the layout decides which sub-steps it writes out on a new number (such as listing the digits before swapping two of them), and a model’s own layout writes out the sub-steps that this model needs. A peer’s example in the reader’s layout is as good as the reader’s own (73.2% vs 73.9%), and the reader’s own sentences in the peer’s layout are as poor as the peer’s (67.3% vs 67.9%). If a model recovers anything of its own computation from text it wrote, it does so through the step structure; the wording adds nothing measurable.

Limitations

  • Scope. One task family and six models of 3.8–9B parameters with thinking off. Two writers of the pilots (Llama-3.1-8B, Swallow-8B) were replaced because they executed the procedures correctly only 4–12% of the time. Readers decode greedily. Larger models and less formulaic reasoning are untested.
  • Uneven across readers. OLMo-3-7B and GLM-4-9B carry most of the effect. InternLM3-8B shows a small reverse effect (−2.9 pp in deployment, −2.4 pp for layout), and neither Japanese reader shows an own advantage in deployment.
  • Recognition is coarse. It is a four-way choice per step and at ceiling for four readers, so equal scores mean the steps were identified equally, not that the examples were understood equally in every respect. The pre-registered dissociation is modest (\(p = 0.034\)) and carried by \(N = 1\); at \(N = 4\) recognition shows an own advantage too (+5.5 pp). The analysis of the four ceiling readers was added by amendment halfway through the follow-up; it holds on the later half alone. A verification task (spot the wrong step in a chain of numbers) was at chance for every reader and is uninformative.
  • Layout rewrites. One rewriting model. The rewrites follow their layout source closely (line-structure similarity 1.00, against 0.40 to the wording source) but carry the wording less cleanly: 73% of source-specific phrases come from the intended source, so the measured \(W\) is about 0.46 of the effect of a complete wording swap. Scaled up, the upper end of its interval still allows at most about +3 pp. Layout includes which sub-steps are written out, so it is not purely typographic. Only \(N = 1\) was tested, and the link between writing digits out and accuracy is correlational.
