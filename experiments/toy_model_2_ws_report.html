Generative learning · toy model 2 · wake–sleep

Dreaming lets a Hebbian learner align its latent space, and beat an autoencoder trained on the same examples

A two-layer network learns a world of six hidden binary causes from unlabelled samples. It starts with the right mean and covariance, but with its latent axes turned about {{two_layer_gen0}} away from the world's. Wake–sleep practice alternates reading supplied samples, which trains generation, with dreaming its own, which trains recognition, each by a local Hebbian rule. Practice turns the axes towards the world's, and a second layer learns which causes go together. Given a fixed number of examples, the learner does best when about 30% of them are its own dreams. It then ends {{b21_ratioAE}} times closer to the world than the same network trained as an autoencoder on supplied samples only. Dreaming starts to pay once the supplied samples include about {{crossRange}} distinct ones, whether the latent space has 16 or 256 configurations.

Summary

  1. The setup. Six hidden ±1 causes, themselves set by two top causes, are rendered linearly in 12 dimensions with noise. The learner is a two-layer Helmholtz machine: a generator and a recognizer in each layer. It starts with a generator that gets every mean and covariance of the data right but whose latent axes are turned, a defect that reconstruction error, principal components and anything else second-order cannot see.
  2. Practice turns the axes, because the causes are binary. With ±1 causes, how well a generator explains the data depends on the orientation of its axes. Wake follows that slope by reading samples through the recognizer; sleep keeps the recognizer matched to the turning generator. With two causes, the axes go from 30° to {{b500}} in 500 updates; with recognition frozen, they stop at {{frozen}}. A linear-Gaussian learner has no such slope, whatever the shape of its latent or noise covariance (box in section 1).
  3. Generation leads, recognition follows. In both layers the recognizer gets within a few degrees of the best recognizer for the current generator after a few hundred updates and stays there ({{lagMin}}–{{lagMax}} at the end). Turning the generator towards the world is the slow process ({{two_layer_gen0}} → {{two_layer_gen}} over {{steps}} updates). Recognition never runs ahead.
  4. A second layer learns the prior. Trained by the same rule on the codes the learner gives its own samples, it learns which causes go together. The learner ends at {{two_layer_kl}} nats, better than the same learner handed the world's true prior ({{true_prior_kl}}) or one with independent causes ({{independent_kl}}). The price is that which unit stands for which cause becomes arbitrary.
  5. At a fixed budget of examples, dreaming beats watching. Out of {{bMax}} examples, wake–sleep does best with about 30% dreamed ({{b21_ws70}} nats). With none dreamed, its recognizer is never trained and it stalls at {{stall}}. The autoencoder baseline, trained end to end on supplied examples, plateaus at {{b21_ae100}}. Wake–sleep is ahead in at least 11 of {{bWorlds}} worlds from {{beatFrom}} examples on, and ties at 213 and below. A learner that computes exact posteriors needs no dreams at all and ends at {{b21_ws_teach100}}, but it sums over all 64 latent configurations for every example.
  6. Dreaming pays from a few dozen distinct examples. With a fixed set of N supplied examples, reused, dreaming helps once N is above about {{crossRange}}. At N = 128, wake–sleep reaches {{n128_ws70}} nats, against {{n128_ws100}} without dreams and {{n128_ae100}} for the autoencoder. The threshold is the same for 16, 64 and 256 latent configurations. Below it, every learner memorises its few examples, and dreams no longer help.
  7. Dreams teach recognition; they do not explore. Replaying the codes that recognition gave to supplied samples works as well as dreaming from the learned prior ({{d21_ws_replay}} vs {{d21_ws}} nats), although it covers fewer latent configurations. Dreaming uniformly over all configurations is much worse ({{d21_ws_uniform}}). Dreams are useful as exactly labelled examples for the recognizer, drawn from the latent distribution the generator is being fitted to.

1. The model

World

Two top causes \(\mathbf{h}\in\{-1,+1\}^2\), uniform, set the probabilities of six causes, \(p(z_i=+1\mid\mathbf{h})=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(2\beta(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{h})_i\big)\), with a random Gaussian \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\beta=1.5\). The six causes are rendered as \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) in 12 dimensions, with random unit-length columns \(\mathbf{a}_j\) and \(\sigma=0.5\). Each world draws its own \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\). The learner never sees a cause.

Learner

Generation runs top-down. Four top causes \(\mathbf{g}\in\{\pm1\}^4\) are uniform (the world has two; the learner is not told), and

\[\begin{gathered}p(z_i=+1\mid\mathbf{g})=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(2(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{a})_i\big),\\ p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})=\mathcal N(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{c},\,\sigma^2\mathbf{I}).\end{gathered}\]

Recognition runs bottom-up, one factorised layer at a time:

\[\begin{gathered}q(z_i=+1\mid\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(2(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})_i\big),\\ q(g_j=+1\mid\mathbf{z})=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(2(\mathbf{R}_2\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_2)_j\big).\end{gathered}\]

Practice: wake and sleep

Wake reads a supplied sample and trains generation. It reads the causes upward, \(\mathbf{z}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\), then \(\mathbf{g}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z})\), and each generative layer learns to predict the layer below:

\[\begin{gathered}\Delta\mathbf{W}=\eta\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{c})\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T},\\ \Delta\mathbf{V}=\eta\,\big(\mathbf{z}-\tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{a})\big)\,\mathbf{g}^{\mathsf T}.\end{gathered}\]

Sleep dreams and trains recognition. It dreams top-down, \(\mathbf{g}\sim p(\mathbf{g})\), \(\mathbf{z}\sim p(\cdot\mid\mathbf{g})\), \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim p(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z})\), and each recognition layer learns to recover the cause behind what it was shown:

\[\begin{gathered}\Delta\mathbf{R}=\eta_s\big(\mathbf{z}-\tanh(\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{b})\big)\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf T},\\ \Delta\mathbf{R}_2=\eta_s\big(\mathbf{g}-\tanh(\mathbf{R}_2\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_2)\big)\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T}.\end{gathered}\]

Rates \(\eta=0.02\) and \(\eta_s=0.2\), batches of {{batch}}. Every update is local and Hebbian. Multiplied out, each is a Hebbian co-activity term minus an anti-Hebbian term in which the receiving unit's activity is replaced by the network's own prediction of it, for a single connection

\[\Delta w_{ij}=\eta\,(x_i-\hat x_i)\,z_j,\qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{c}.\]

This is the delta rule: it needs the activity of the sending unit and, at the receiving unit, the difference between what arrives from below and what arrives from above. No error travels through other layers, and no weight is copied between the pathways. In sleep, the Hebbian half alone already builds the transpose of the generator (for causes of mean zero, \(\mathbb E[\mathbf{z}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf T}]=\boldsymbol\Sigma_{\mathbf z}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\)), so recognition learns to read each cause along the direction in which generation writes it, and the anti-Hebbian half removes the cross-talk.

The start: right statistics, wrong axes

Every learner starts from the same generator. It matches the data's mean and covariance exactly, but its axes are the world's turned by a random rotation \(\mathbf{Q}\): \(\mathbf{W}_0=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1/2}\). Its recognizer has been fitted to this generator by sleep, so it reads its own dreams well and the world's samples badly. Nothing that looks only at means and covariances can tell this generator from the right one.

Why binary causes, and not a linear-Gaussian model. Give the causes a Gaussian prior \(\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)\) with any covariance, round or elongated, and the noise any Gaussian covariance \(\boldsymbol\Psi\), round or not. The data are then Gaussian too,

\[p(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal N\big(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf{c},\;\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}+\boldsymbol\Psi\big),\]

and replacing \(\mathbf{W}\) by \(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1/2}\), for any rotation \(\mathbf{Q}\) (with \(\mathbf{c}\) shifted to keep the mean), leaves this distribution unchanged. Every rotation of the latent axes, measured in the prior's whitened coordinates, explains the data equally well, so no rule that improves the likelihood can prefer the world's axes. An elongated prior or elongated noise changes which rotations these are, not that they exist. In the previous version of this report, the linear-Gaussian twin of this learner (one layer, with the binary prior's mean and covariance) went from {{gauss_gen0}} to {{gauss_gen}}. A factorised recognizer adds a small preference for axes along which the posterior factorises, but that is set by \(\mathbf{W}\) and \(\boldsymbol\Psi\), not by the world. Only a non-Gaussian prior breaks the symmetry, and ±1 causes are the simplest one.

2. Two causes: why practice turns the axes

With two causes, misalignment is a single angle. The world's samples (grey) sit in four clusters at \((\pm1,\pm1)\), so the world's axes are the horizontal and the vertical. Why the axes turn

Write \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}\) for what the generator's axes see in a sample, and \(\varphi(u)=\mathbb E[z\mid u]\) for the posterior mean of a cause. Put the exact posterior into the wake update and keep only the part that rotates \(\mathbf{W}\). The rotation speed is then

\[\dot\theta\;\propto\;\tfrac12\,\mathbb E\big[\varphi(u_1)\,u_2-\varphi(u_2)\,u_1\big],\qquad \varphi(u)=\tanh(u/\sigma^2).\]

The bracket is non-zero and pulls \(\theta\) to the nearest multiple of 90°. This is the rotation step of independent component analysis, with the nonlinearity supplied by the learner's own prior: ICA has to choose that nonlinearity by hand, here sleep makes the recognizer compute it for the current generator. With Gaussian causes the posterior mean is linear, \(\varphi(u)\propto u\), and the bracket vanishes at every angle, which is the two-cause face of the symmetry in the box above.

The right-hand chart shows the same as a landscape. With an exact recognizer, the wake update follows the gradient of the log-likelihood (Fisher's identity), and the landscape has valleys at the world's axes: a generator turned by 15° pays {{land15}} nats per sample, by 30° {{land30}}, by 45° {{land45}}.

Why sleep is needed. Wake measures the slope through the recognizer. If the recognizer is frozen, the slope is measured in the recognizer's old coordinates, and the generator stops partway: started at 30°, it ends at {{frozen}} ({{frozenRange}} over 16 runs). With sleep, the recognizer trails the generator by about {{lag250}} at update 250, and the axes are at {{b500}} after 500 updates and {{b1000}} after 1,000.

3. Six causes: generation leads, recognition follows

With six causes, misalignment is measured as angles between weight vectors, averaged over units. {{worlds}} worlds, {{N}} stored samples, one wake and one sleep batch per update.

  • Generator against the world: each generator column \(\mathbf{w}_i\) against the world column \(\mathbf{a}_j\) it is matched to (the best one-to-one matching, ignoring signs). This is the rotation of the latent space.
  • Recognizer against its generator: sleep is a logistic regression of dreamed causes on dreams, so for every generator there is a recognizer \(\mathbf{R}^*(\mathbf{W})\) that sleep would converge to. The angle between the rows of \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(\mathbf{R}^*\) measures how far recognition lags behind generation. The top layer is treated the same way, \(\mathbf{R}_2\) against \(\mathbf{R}_2^*(\mathbf{V})\), fitted exactly over all \(2^4\times2^6\) configurations.

Mean ± 1 standard error over {{worlds}} worlds. Dashed: the learned-prior learner's top recognizer, from update 200 (it starts at zero). The learned-prior and independent learners start with a large lag because their recognizer was fitted under the world's prior, while their own prior starts out nearly independent.

  • Recognition aligns with generation fast, and stays aligned. The learned-prior learner's recognizer is within {{lag200}} of its target by update 200, and the blank top recognizer within {{toplag600}} by update 600. At the end, every recognizer in every world and condition is {{lagMin}}–{{lagMax}} from its target (learned prior: {{two_layer_lag}} bottom, {{two_layer_top}} top).
  • Generation aligns with the world slowly. The learned-prior learner's axes turn from {{two_layer_gen0}} to {{two_layer_gen}} over the whole run (worlds range from {{two_range}}). The stored set limits how far: with unlimited fresh samples they reach {{two_layer_fgen}}.
  • Reading the world improves as a consequence. Since the recognizer tracks the generator, it reads the world's samples better as the generator turns: the world's causes, in the learner's own units, come out right {{two_layer_acc0}} → {{two_layer_acc}} of the time, the learner's own dreams {{own0}} → {{own}}.
  • So recognition follows; it never gets ahead. The specification hoped recognition would stay ahead of generation. Here the generator leads, through wake, and the recognizer catches up, through sleep, in both layers.

4. A second layer learns the prior

The one-layer toy had to be handed its prior: it knew in advance which of its causes tend to occur together. A top layer learns this instead. In wake it watches the codes its recognizer assigns to the stored samples and learns their co-occurrences, by the same Hebbian rule. In sleep it is where dreams start, so it decides which combinations of causes get dreamed.

Mean ± 1 standard error over {{worlds}} worlds. Right: root-mean-square difference between the correlations of the learner's prior, re-expressed in world order through the generator's current units, and the correlations of the world's causes. Jumps come from the matching of units to causes switching while a learner is still misaligned.

  • Independent causes ({{independent_kl}} nats; axes {{independent_gen}} off). The learner's prior says its causes are independent, so practice turns its axes towards directions in which the codes look independent. The world's causes are correlated, so those directions mix them, as ICA does with dependent sources.
  • The true prior, given ({{true_prior_kl}} nats; {{true_prior_gen}}). The correlations are right, but each unit must also be bound to the right slot of the prior, and a wrong binding is a stable state of practice. In the world's order, the fixed prior then misstates which rendered causes go together (correlation error {{true_prior_corr}}).
  • Learned prior ({{two_layer_kl}} nats; {{two_layer_gen}}). The top layer learns whatever correlations the codes have, in whatever unit order the bottom layer happens to use. That frees the bottom layer to turn further towards the world's axes (correlation error {{two_layer_corr}}). The top layer turns towards the world's two top causes as well, from {{top_world0}} to {{top_world}}. With two top units instead of four, the learner ends at {{two_layer_h2_kl}} nats.

What the top layer converges to

The top layer's wake update stops changing when, averaged over stored samples \(\mathbf{x}\), their codes \(\mathbf{z}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\) and top codes \(\mathbf{g}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z})\),

\[\mathbb E\Big[\big(\mathbf{z}-\tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{a})\big)\,\mathbf{g}^{\mathsf T}\Big]=\mathbf{0}.\]

This is one wake–sleep step one layer up, with the codes as its data. The top layer is therefore a generative model of the aggregate posterior, the distribution of codes the learner assigns to its stored samples,

\[\bar q(\mathbf{z})=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}_n),\]

which, for a fixed recognizer, is exactly the prior that maximises the likelihood bound \(\sum_n\mathbb E_{q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}_n)}[\log p(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{z})]\). It gets the world's correlations right only once the bottom units are the world's causes, which is why the right-hand chart falls together with the angle in section 3. Section 7 comes back to this: dreams from \(\bar q\) are, in effect, replays of the codes given to the data.

The price: labels become arbitrary

A learned prior fits whatever unit order the bottom layer chose, so the learner's unit 3 need not be the world's cause 3. In earlier runs of the same model ({{priorWorlds}} worlds, {{N}} samples), the learner reads the world's causes in the world's own labels (up to a global sign flip) with {{priorTopSym}} bit accuracy with a learned top layer, against {{priorFixedSym}} with the true prior given; 50% is chance. For an own-versus-foreign question, label identity matters, so a learned prior trades it for a better generator.

5. How much to dream: a fixed budget of examples new

Sections 3 and 4 reuse {{N}} stored samples and dream as often as they read. Here the learner gets a fixed number \(P\) of examples in all. A share \(x\) of them are supplied: fresh world samples, each used once, which train generation by wake. The rest are its own dreams, each used once, which train recognition by sleep. Supplied and dreamed batches are interleaved so that every prefix of training has the same share, so one run gives the whole learning curve. The baselines get the same budgets; for them too, dreams train recognition by sleep, so \(x=100\%\) means no generation at all. {{bWorlds}} worlds; every learner starts from the rotated generator of section 1 at {{bStart}} nats.

7. What the dreams are for new

One reading of section 5 is that dreams explore: they visit latent configurations the supplied examples never showed. A control changes only where the bottom layer's dreams take their latents from, at 70% supplied with fresh examples. Plain wake–sleep dreams them from its learned prior. Replay takes the codes recognition gave to the last 1,024 supplied examples, so it never goes beyond the current explanations of the data. Uniform draws independent, uniform latents: maximal exploration, ignoring the prior. The top layer sleeps on its own dreams throughout.

Mean ± 1 standard error over {{dWorlds}} worlds.

  • Replay is as good as dreaming from the prior, or better. At 216 examples: {{d16_ws_replay}} vs {{d16_ws}} nats, replay lower in {{d16_replayWins}} of {{dWorlds}} worlds; at 221: {{d21_ws_replay}} vs {{d21_ws}}. Yet replayed latents are less varied (entropy {{d16_ws_replay_ent}} vs {{d16_ws_ent}} nats at 216). Widening the replayed dreams with extra rendering noise, until they spread as far in \(\mathbf{x}\) as dreams from the prior, changes nothing ({{d16_ws_replay_wide}}).
  • Uniform latents are much worse, {{d21_ws_uniform}} nats at 221 and worse than the prior's dreams in {{d21_uniformWins}} of {{dWorlds}} worlds, although their entropy is the maximum ({{uniformEnt}} nats).
  • With a fixed prior, only the prior's dreams help. In the one-layer learner with the true prior given ({{oneN}} stored samples, {{oneWorlds}} worlds), from {{one_start}} nats: prior dreams {{one_prior}}, no dreams {{one_none}}, uniform {{one_uniform}}, replay {{one_replay}}.

Sleep trains the recognizer on dreams whose causes are known exactly, and what matters is that those causes follow the distribution the generator is being fitted to. With a learned top layer, that distribution is the aggregate posterior, the codes recognition assigns to the data (section 4), so replaying those codes is the same thing with less sampling noise. With a fixed prior, replay only re-teaches the recognizer its own current readings, mistakes included, and dreams from the prior are what pulls them back. Uniform latents teach the recognizer the posterior of the wrong prior.

9. What this shows, and what it doesn't

Shows

  • A rotation of the latent space that no second-order statistic can see is undone by wake–sleep practice, because binary causes make the likelihood depend on orientation. Gaussian causes of any covariance do not.
  • Local Hebbian updates suffice: wake turns generation, sleep keeps recognition matched to it, in every layer. A top layer trained by the same rule learns the prior and beats a learner handed the true one.
  • At a fixed number of examples, spending about 30% on dreams beats spending none, and beats the autoencoding baseline, from {{beatFrom}} examples and from about {{crossRange}} distinct supplied examples on, for 16 to 256 latent configurations.
  • Dreams act as exactly labelled training examples for recognition, drawn from the right latent distribution, not as exploration.

Does not show

  • That dreaming is necessary for every learner: one that computes exact posteriors needs none, and is {{b16_ratioTeach}} times better at 216 examples. It sums over all latent configurations, which does not scale.
  • Perfect alignment: with {{N}} stored samples the axes stop at {{two_layer_gen}} on average.
  • Correct labels: with a learned prior, which unit is which cause is arbitrary.
  • Recognition running ahead of generation, or an own-versus-foreign advantage once aligned.
  • Generality: one family of worlds (six binary causes from two top causes, rendered linearly in 12 dimensions), and a start that already hands the learner the world's subspace; a small random start is untested. The autoencoder's recognition rate was inherited from the first toy, not tuned.
