Generative learning · toy model 2 · wake–sleep

Practice turns a rotated latent space towards the world's, and a second layer learns the prior

A learner has {{N}} unlabelled samples from a world with six hidden binary causes. Its generator starts with the right mean and covariance, but its latent axes are turned away from the world's by about {{chain_two_layer_gen0}}. Wake–sleep practice, which alternates reading the stored samples and dreaming its own, turns the axes towards the world's. Every weight learns by a Hebbian rule: the activity at one end of a connection times the error at the other. A second layer, trained by the same rule, learns which causes go together. The learner ends with a better generator than one that was handed the world's true prior. The linear-Gaussian version of the same learner has nothing to turn towards, and its axes wander.

Summary

  1. The question. The learner's generator gets every mean and covariance of the data right, but its latent axes are turned. Can wake–sleep practice on a fixed set of foreign samples undo that rotation? And can a learned second layer replace the prior that the one-layer version had to be given?
  2. Every update is Hebbian. Each weight changes by the activity at one end of its connection times the error at the other: a Hebbian co-activity term minus an anti-Hebbian term in which the network's own prediction stands in for the real activity. Wake trains generation this way on stored samples, sleep trains recognition this way on dreams, in both layers.
  3. The rotation is undone, because the causes are binary. With ±1 causes, how well the generator explains the data depends on the orientation of its latent axes, with the best fit at the world's axes. Wake reads stored samples through the recognizer and turns the generator towards that best fit. Sleep then brings the recognizer after it. With two causes, the axes turn from 30° to {{b500}} within 500 updates. With recognition frozen they stop at {{frozen}}.
  4. Recognition follows generation closely; generation reaches the world slowly. In every layer, the recognizer comes within about ten degrees of the best recognizer for the current generator after a few hundred updates, and stays there ({{lagMin}}–{{lagMax}} at the end, in every condition). The generator's turn towards the world takes the whole run. Recognition never runs ahead of generation.
  5. A second layer explains the prior. The world's causes are correlated. A one-layer learner with independent latents cannot represent that, and settles at a mixed orientation ({{chain_independent_gen}} off, {{chain_independent_kl}} nats). A top layer trained on the learner's own codes, by the same Hebbian rule, learns the correlations, and the bottom layer is then free to turn towards the world's axes ({{chain_two_layer_gen}}, {{chain_two_layer_kl}} nats). This beats a learner handed the true prior ({{chain_true_prior_kl}} nats), which must also bind each of its units to the right slot of that prior. The price is that which unit stands for which cause becomes arbitrary.
  6. The linear-Gaussian twin has nothing to practise. With Gaussian causes of the same mean and covariance, every rotation explains the data equally well, so wake never turns the axes. They drift at random ({{chain_gaussian_gen0}} → {{chain_gaussian_gen}}). Its error grows only by fitting the sampling noise of the stored set ({{chain_gaussian_kl0}} → {{chain_gaussian_kl}} nats; {{chain_gaussian_fkl}} with fresh samples). A Gaussian second layer would only add a covariance, which the first layer can absorb anyway.

The model

World

Six hidden causes \(\mathbf{z}\in\{-1,+1\}^6\) are rendered as \(\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) in 12 dimensions, with random unit-length columns \(\mathbf{a}_j\) of \(\mathbf{A}\) and \(\sigma=0.5\). In the chain world, neighbouring causes agree with probability 0.85. In the two-layer world, two hidden top causes set the probabilities of the six. The learner keeps {{N}} samples and never sees a cause.

Learner

Two-layer learner (binary causes)

Generation runs top-down. Four top causes \(\mathbf{g}\in\{\pm1\}^4\) are uniform, and

\[\begin{gathered}p(z_i=+1\mid\mathbf{g})=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(2(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{a})_i\big),\\ p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})=\mathcal N(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{c},\,\sigma^2\mathbf{I}).\end{gathered}\]

Recognition runs bottom-up, one factorised layer at a time:

\[\begin{gathered}q(z_i=+1\mid\mathbf{x})=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(2(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b})_i\big),\\ q(g_j=+1\mid\mathbf{z})=\operatorname{sigmoid}\big(2(\mathbf{R}_2\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_2)_j\big).\end{gathered}\]

Linear-Gaussian twin

Its causes are Gaussian with exactly the mean \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and covariance \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\) of the world's binary prior, and it has no top layer:

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma),\\ p(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})=\mathcal N(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{c},\,\sigma^2\mathbf{I}),\\ q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})=\mathcal N\big(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b},\,\operatorname{diag}\mathbf{s}^2\big).\end{gathered}\]

This is factor analysis learned by delta-rule wake–sleep (Neal & Dayan, 1997). Everything else, including the start, the data and the rates, is the same.

Practice: wake and sleep

Wake: read a stored sample, train generation

Read the causes upward, \(\mathbf{z}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})\), then \(\mathbf{g}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z})\). Each generative layer learns to predict the layer below:

\[\begin{gathered}\Delta\mathbf{W}=\eta\,(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{c})\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T},\\ \Delta\mathbf{V}=\eta\,\big(\mathbf{z}-\tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{a})\big)\,\mathbf{g}^{\mathsf T}.\end{gathered}\]

Sleep: dream, train recognition

Dream top-down, \(\mathbf{g}\sim p(\mathbf{g})\), \(\mathbf{z}\sim p(\cdot\mid\mathbf{g})\), \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\sim p(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z})\). Each recognition layer learns to recover the cause behind what it was shown:

\[\begin{gathered}\Delta\mathbf{R}=\eta_s\big(\mathbf{z}-\tanh(\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{b})\big)\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf T},\\ \Delta\mathbf{R}_2=\eta_s\big(\mathbf{g}-\tanh(\mathbf{R}_2\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{b}_2)\big)\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T}.\end{gathered}\]

Rates \(\eta=0.02\) and \(\eta_s=0.2\), batches of 32, {{steps}} updates, each one wake and one sleep batch. The Gaussian twin uses the same rules with the identity in place of \(\tanh\).

Why these are Hebbian rules

Multiply out the brackets. Every update is a Hebbian term, the product of the activities at the two ends of a connection, minus an anti-Hebbian term in which the receiving unit's activity is replaced by the network's own prediction of it:

\[\Delta\mathbf{W}=\eta\,\underbrace{\mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T}}_{\text{Hebbian}}-\eta\,\underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{x}}\,\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T}}_{\text{anti-Hebbian}},\qquad\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{c},\]
\[\Delta\mathbf{R}=\eta_s\,\underbrace{\mathbf{z}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf T}}_{\text{Hebbian}}-\eta_s\,\underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{z}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf T}}_{\text{anti-Hebbian}},\qquad\hat{\mathbf{z}}=\tanh(\mathbf{R}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\mathbf{b}),\]

and the same one layer up for \(\mathbf{V}\) and \(\mathbf{R}_2\). For a single connection, from cause unit \(j\) to sample unit \(i\) (highlighted in the diagram above),

\[\Delta w_{ij}=\eta\,(x_i-\hat x_i)\,z_j .\]

It needs the activity \(z_j\) of the sending unit, and at the receiving unit the difference between what arrives from below (the sample) and what arrives from above (the prediction). Both are available at the two ends of the connection. No error travels through other layers, and no weight is copied from one pathway to the other. This is the Widrow–Hoff delta rule: Hebbian learning made stable by subtracting the network's own prediction.

What each half does:

  • In wake, the Hebbian term \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T}\) strengthens connections between the causes the recognizer read out and the sample units that were active. The generator stops changing when its predictions reproduce those co-activities, \(\mathbb E[\hat{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T}]=\mathbb E[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}^{\mathsf T}]\).
  • In sleep, the Hebbian term alone already builds the transpose of the generator. For causes of mean zero, averaged over dreams, \(\mathbb E[\mathbf{z}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\mathsf T}]=\boldsymbol\Sigma_{\mathbf z}\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\), so recognition starts out reading each cause along the direction in which generation writes it. The anti-Hebbian term then removes the cross-talk between causes. Recognition gets aligned with generation from the learner's own dreams, without weight transport.

The start: right statistics, wrong axes

Every learner starts from the same generator. It matches the data's mean and covariance exactly, but its axes are the world's turned by a random rotation \(\mathbf{Q}\): \(\mathbf{W}_0=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{1/2}\mathbf{Q}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1/2}\). Its recognizer has been fitted to this generator by sleep, so it reads its own dreams well and the world's samples badly. Nothing that looks only at means and covariances can tell this generator from the right one, so reconstruction, principal components and linear autoencoders are already at their optimum here.

Why the binary learner turns and the Gaussian one does not

Write \(\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}\) for what the generator's axes see in a sample, and \(\varphi(u)=\mathbb E[z\mid u]\) for the posterior mean of a cause. Put the exact posterior into the wake update and keep only the part that rotates \(\mathbf{W}\). The rotation speed is then

\[\dot\theta\;\propto\;\tfrac12\,\mathbb E\big[\varphi(u_1)\,u_2-\varphi(u_2)\,u_1\big].\]

Binary causes: \(\varphi(u)=\tanh(u/\sigma^2)\)

The bracket is non-zero and pulls \(\theta\) to the nearest multiple of 90°. This is the rotation step of independent component analysis, with the nonlinearity supplied by the learner's own prior. ICA has to choose that nonlinearity by hand; here sleep makes the recognizer compute it for the current generator.

Gaussian causes: \(\varphi(u)=u/(1+\sigma^2)\)

The posterior mean is linear, so the bracket is \(\mathbb E[u_1u_2-u_2u_1]/(1+\sigma^2)=0\) at every angle. More generally, \(p(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal N(\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf{c},\,\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}+\sigma^2\mathbf{I})\) is unchanged by every rotation of the latent axes, so no rule that improves the likelihood can prefer one.

The right-hand chart shows the same thing as a landscape. With an exact recognizer, the wake update follows the gradient of the log-likelihood (Fisher's identity). For binary causes the landscape has valleys at the world's axes, and a generator turned by 30° pays {{land30}} nats per sample. The Gaussian landscape is flat.

Why sleep is needed. Wake measures the slope through the recognizer. If the recognizer is frozen, the slope is measured in the recognizer's old coordinates, and the generator stops partway. Started at 30°, it ends at {{frozen}} ({{frozenRange}} over 16 runs). With sleep, the recognizer trails the generator by about {{lag250}} at update 250, and the axes reach {{b1000}} after 1,000 updates. The Gaussian twin over the same 16 runs ends anywhere between {{grange}} (mean {{g3000}}), with its recognizer {{grec}} from its generator: the two stay aligned with each other, but neither aligns with the world.

Six causes: how recognition and generation align, layer by layer

With six causes, misalignment is measured as angles between weight vectors, averaged over units:

  • Generator against the world: each generator column \(\mathbf{w}_i\) against the world column \(\mathbf{a}_j\) it is matched to (the best one-to-one matching, ignoring signs). This is the rotation of the latent space.
  • Recognizer against its generator: sleep is logistic regression of dreamed causes on dreams. For every generator there is therefore a recognizer \(\mathbf{R}^*(\mathbf{W})\) that sleep would converge to, and the angle between the rows of \(\mathbf{R}\) and \(\mathbf{R}^*\) measures how far recognition lags behind generation. The top layer is treated the same way, \(\mathbf{R}_2\) against \(\mathbf{R}_2^*(\mathbf{V})\), fitted exactly over all \(2^4\times2^6\) configurations.

  • Recognition aligns with generation fast, and stays aligned. In the chain world, the two-layer learner's recognizer is within {{chain_lag200}} of its target by update 200. The blank top recognizer is within {{chain_toplag600}} by update 600.   • Generation aligns with the world slowly. The two-layer learner's axes turn from {{chain_two_layer_gen0}} to {{chain_two_layer_gen}} over the whole run (worlds range from {{chain_two_range}}). The stored set limits how far: with unlimited fresh samples they reach {{chain_two_layer_fgen}}.
  • Recognition of the world follows. Since the recognizer tracks the generator, it reads the world's samples better as the generator turns. Read in terms of the learner's own units, the world's causes come out right {{chain_two_layer_acc0}} → {{chain_two_layer_acc}} of the time; the learner's own dreams, {{chain_two_layer_own0}} → {{chain_two_layer_own}}.
  • So recognition follows generation; it never gets ahead. The specification hoped recognition would stay ahead of generation. Here the generator leads, through wake, and the recognizer catches up, through sleep, in both layers.

How a second layer explains the prior

The one-layer toy had to be handed its prior: it knew in advance which of its concepts tend to occur together. A top layer can learn this instead. In wake it watches the codes its recognizer assigns to the stored samples, and learns their co-occurrences by the same Hebbian rule, \(\Delta\mathbf{V}=\eta\,\mathbf{z}\mathbf{g}^{\mathsf T}-\eta\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{g}^{\mathsf T}\). In sleep it is where dreams start, so it decides which combinations of causes get dreamed. The bottom layer's sleep is therefore trained on whatever correlations the codes of the stored samples show. The comparison with the other learners shows why that matters.

  • Independent causes ({{chain_independent_kl}} nats; axes {{chain_independent_gen}} off). The learner's prior says its causes are independent, so practice turns its axes towards directions in which the codes look independent. The world's causes are correlated, so those directions mix them, as ICA does on dependent sources. The axes stop far from the world's.
  • The true prior, given ({{chain_true_prior_kl}} nats; {{chain_true_prior_gen}}). The correlations are right, but each unit must also be bound to the right slot of the prior: the generator's unit 3 has to render the cause the prior calls 3. A wrong binding is a stable state of practice, as the write-up shows. In the world's order, the fixed prior then misstates which rendered causes go together (correlation error {{chain_true_prior_corr}}).
  • Two layers ({{chain_two_layer_kl}} nats; {{chain_two_layer_gen}}). The top layer learns whatever correlations the codes have, in whatever order the bottom layer happens to use. That frees the bottom layer to turn much further towards the world's axes (correlation error {{chain_two_layer_corr}}).

What the top layer converges to

The top layer's wake update stops changing when

\[\mathbb E_{\mathbf{x}\sim\text{stored}}\;\mathbb E_{\mathbf{z}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x})}\;\mathbb E_{\mathbf{g}\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{z})}\Big[\big(\mathbf{z}-\tanh(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{a})\big)\,\mathbf{g}^{\mathsf T}\Big]=\mathbf{0}.\]

This is one wake–sleep step one layer up, with the codes \(\mathbf{z}\) as its data. The top layer is therefore a generative model of the aggregate posterior, the distribution of codes the learner assigns to its stored samples:

\[\bar q(\mathbf{z})=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}_n).\]

For a fixed recognizer, \(\bar q\) is exactly the prior that maximises the likelihood bound \(\sum_n\mathbb E_{q(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}_n)}[\log p(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{z})]\). Learning the top layer is learning the prior the data call for, in the learner's own coordinates. It can only get the world's correlations right once the bottom units are the world's causes, which is why the right-hand chart falls together with the angle above.

In the two-layer world, which has two top causes of its own, the learner's top layer turns towards them as well, from {{deep_top_world0}} to {{deep_top_world}} (angle between the learner's top-layer weights, re-expressed in world order, and the world's).

Why a Gaussian second layer adds nothing

Stack a Gaussian layer on the twin: \(\mathbf{g}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\) and \(\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{g}+\mathbf{a}+\tau\boldsymbol\epsilon\). The causes then only get a covariance \(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf T}+\tau^2\mathbf{I}\), and the data a covariance \(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf T}+\tau^2\mathbf{I})\mathbf{W}^{\mathsf T}+\sigma^2\mathbf{I}\), which the first layer alone can produce with \(\mathbf{W}'=\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\mathsf T}+\tau^2\mathbf{I})^{1/2}\). A linear-Gaussian stack is still one linear-Gaussian model. How it splits between layers is not identifiable, and neither is the orientation of either layer. The binary top layer is different: a mixture of \(2^4\) factorised distributions over \(\pm1\) causes cannot be absorbed into \(\mathbf{W}\), so it has something of its own to learn.

The price: labels become arbitrary

A learned prior fits whatever unit order the bottom layer chose, so the learner's unit 3 need not be the world's cause 3. In the earlier runs of the same model ({{priorWorlds}} chain worlds, {{N}} samples), the learner reads the world's causes in the world's own labels (up to the prior's symmetries) with {{priorTopSym}} bit accuracy with a learned top layer, against {{priorFixedSym}} with the true prior given. For the project's own-versus-foreign question, label identity matters, so a learned prior trades it for a better generator.

Two top units instead of four: {{chain_two_layer_h2_kl}} nats in the chain world, {{deep_two_layer_h2_kl}} in the two-layer world (four units: {{chain_two_layer_kl}} and {{deep_two_layer_kl}}). In the two-layer world, the learners end at {{deep_two_layer_kl}} (two layers), {{deep_true_prior_kl}} (true prior given), {{deep_independent_kl}} (independent causes) and {{deep_gaussian_kl}} nats (Gaussian twin).

The Gaussian twin starts with a lower error than the binary learner: a single Gaussian with the right covariance is a better density than 64 clusters in the wrong places. It cannot improve on that, while the binary learner ends far below it. Its small rise with stored samples is overfitting of a 12-dimensional covariance to {{N}} samples. Its recognizer does reorganise somewhat, because a factorised recognizer prefers generators whose posterior is factorised, but that preference has nothing to do with the world's axes.

What this shows, and what it doesn't

Shows

  • A rotation of the latent space that no second-order statistic can see is undone by wake–sleep practice on a fixed sample, because binary causes make the likelihood depend on orientation. Gaussian causes do not.
  • Purely local, Hebbian updates suffice: wake turns generation, sleep keeps recognition aligned with generation, in every layer. Together they turn the axes; either alone does not.
  • A top layer trained by the same rule learns the prior from the learner's own codes, and frees the bottom layer to align. The learner then beats one handed the true prior.

Does not show

  • That practice is necessary: learners that evaluate the prior's density (VAE, exact EM) reach similar errors in the one-layer toy, and have not been run with a learned prior.
  • Perfect alignment: with {{N}} stored samples the axes stop at {{chain_two_layer_gen}} on average, {{chain_two_layer_fgen}} with fresh samples.
  • Correct labels: with a learned prior, which unit is which cause is arbitrary.
  • Recognition running ahead of generation.
  • An own-versus-foreign advantage: once aligned, the learner reads every correct sample equally well.

Toy model 1 (the ring) showed practice escaping a bad optimum of a single-step objective. Here the objective is the likelihood itself, and the defect is a symmetry that second-order statistics cannot break. In both, the signal comes from the learner's own other half: there a checker, here a recognizer.
