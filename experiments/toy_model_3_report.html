Generative learning · toy model 3 · practice on a chain

A learner that can check a chain long before it can write one, and closes the gap by practice

The task is to write chains of {{n}} states in which every state fits both of its neighbours. Checking a chain needs one table of allowed pairs. Writing a chain in a single pass needs a tree of tables that say, for the two ends of a span, which state lies in the middle. A learner made of such tables fills its pair table from a few hundred teacher chains and needs about {{lead3}} times as many for its top table. Practice closes that gap without further teacher chains: the learner writes spans itself, its pair table judges them, and one signed rule counts an entry up when its span passes and down when it fails. The same rule removes the random entries with which the generator starts. Teacher chains never do.

1. Task, teacher and learner

Task

There are \(S\) states. The world is a matrix \(\mathbf{T}\in\{0,1\}^{S\times S}\) with \(T_{ab}=1\) if state \(b\) may follow state \(a\). Every state has \(m\) allowed successors. Here \(S\) = {{S}} and \(m\) = {{m}}, and \(\mathbf{T}\) is drawn at random. A chain is \(\mathbf{x}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)\) with \(n=2^L+1\) states; here \(L=3\), so \(n\) = {{n}}. The population is the valid chains, with a uniform first state and a uniform choice among the allowed steps:

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf{x}\ \text{is valid}\iff\prod_{t=1}^{n-1}T_{x_t x_{t+1}}=1,\\ p(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{S}\prod_{t=1}^{n-1}\frac{T_{x_t x_{t+1}}}{m}.\end{gathered}\]

Two things are asked of a learner.

  • Read. Say whether a given chain is valid.
  • Write. Given the two ends \((a,b)\) of a span of \(2k\) links taken from a population chain (\(k=1,2,4\)), produce the states in between in one pass: a fixed number of steps, in which nothing that was written is fed back in. The span counts as written if the result is valid.

Teacher

The teacher is the ground truth. It reads with \(\mathbf{T}\) itself, writes with the exact tables given below, and shows the learner chains drawn independently from \(p\). Cost is counted in tokens: one token is one state shown by the teacher or written by the learner.

Learner

The learner consists of tables of counts and one rule.

  • Recogniser. A matrix \(\mathbf{C}\): \(C_{ab}\) is the number of times \(b\) followed \(a\) in the teacher's chains. The learner accepts a link \(a\to b\) if \(C_{ab}\ge 1\). Its verdict on a chain or span is \(v=+1\) if it accepts every link and \(v=-1\) otherwise. This is one look-up per link, at any length.
  • Generator. Tables \(n_k(a,b,c)\ge 0\) for \(k=1,2,4\). The entry \((a,b,c)\) of table \(k\) says that \(c\) is the middle state of a span of \(2k\) links from \(a\) to \(b\). To write the span \((a,b)\), a node draws \(c\) in proportion to its counts (below), or uniformly among all states if it holds no entry for \((a,b)\). It hands \((a,c)\) and \((c,b)\) to two nodes of table \(k/2\). Three levels write the {{n}} states in one top-down pass.
\[q_k(c\mid a,b)=\frac{n_k(a,b,c)}{\sum_{c'}n_k(a,b,c')}\]

The teacher's tables, which write the population exactly, are

\[\begin{gathered}p_k(c\mid a,b)=\frac{(\mathbf{T}^{k})_{ac}\,(\mathbf{T}^{k})_{cb}}{(\mathbf{T}^{2k})_{ab}},\\ (\mathbf{T}^{2k})_{ab}=\sum_c(\mathbf{T}^{k})_{ac}\,(\mathbf{T}^{k})_{cb},\end{gathered}\]

where \((\mathbf{T}^{k})_{ac}\) is the number of routes of \(k\) links from \(a\) to \(c\). To read, the learner has to learn \(\mathbf{T}\) itself, which has \(Sm\) = {{pairs_total}} allowed pairs. To write in one pass it has to learn the powers of \(\mathbf{T}\): table \(k\) has about \(Sm^{2k}\) spans to cover, which is {{spans1}}, {{spans2}} and {{spans_total}}.

One rule. An entry is tested when the span it stands for is written out and the recogniser gives its verdict \(v\). Then

\[\begin{gathered}n_k(a,b,c)\;\leftarrow\;\max\{0,\;n_k(a,b,c)+v\},\\ v\in\{+1,-1\}.\end{gathered}\]

Three things put entries to the test.

  • Watching. A teacher chain first adds its links to \(\mathbf{C}\). Each span it contains, with \(a=x_i\), \(c=x_{i+k}\) and \(b=x_{i+2k}\), is then an entry tested on the teacher's own writing. A teacher who makes no slips always passes.
  • Joining. The learner draws a unit of \(k\) links that it holds, \(a\to c\), and a second one that starts where the first ends, \(c\to b\), each in proportion to its count. A unit of one link is an accepted pair. A unit of \(k\) links is the pair of ends of an entry of table \(k/2\). The entry tested is \((a,b,c)\) of table \(k\), and the learner writes its \(2k+1\) states with its lower tables.
  • Rehearsing. The learner draws an entry it holds, in proportion to its count, and writes its span.

Joining and rehearsing use no teacher data. Together they are practice; each episode picks one of the three tables at random.

Start. \(\mathbf{C}=0\). In sections 2 and 3 the generator's tables start empty and practice consists of joins only. In section 4 every table starts with random entries, and practice is half joins and half rehearsals.

Scores. All scores are medians or means over 16 random worlds, on 8,000 held-out population chains each. A population span is written if one pass from its ends gives a valid chain, judged by \(\mathbf{T}\). An own chain is a span of 8 links whose entry the learner draws from its own top table.

2. Watching alone: the recogniser leads

Here the learner only watches. Both of its parts learn from the same chains by the same rule. After \(N\) chains a given allowed pair has been seen with probability \(1-e^{-N(n-1)/(Sm)}\), and a given span of \(2k\) links with probability \(1-e^{-N(n-2k)/(Sm^{2k})}\), because a chain holds \(n-1\) links but only \(n-2k\) such spans, and there are \(m^{2k-1}\) times as many spans as pairs. So table \(k\) trails the pair table by the factor

\[\frac{N^{(k)}_{1/2}}{N^{(0)}_{1/2}}=m^{2k-1}\,\frac{n-1}{n-2k},\]

which is {{lead_theory1}}, {{lead_theory2}} and {{lead_theory3}} for spans of 2, 4 and 8 links.

  • The recogniser is ahead on equal footing. The learner accepts half of the population's links after {{half_read0}} teacher chains. It writes half of the spans of 2, 4 and 8 links after {{half_watch1}}, {{half_watch2}} and {{half_watch3}} chains, which is {{lead1}}, {{lead2}} and {{lead3}} times later.
  • Nothing favours it except that it has less to learn. The delay follows the counting factor in four other worlds as well (right panel).

3. Practice closes the gap, level by level

A join composes two spans that the learner holds into one that it does not. This samples the second formula of section 1, so practice derives the upper tables from the pair table and needs no teacher data.

  • Practice replaces teacher chains. After 4,096 teacher chains the learner accepts every link but writes only {{n12_w3}} of the population's spans of 8 links. Practice alone brings that to {{n12_p3}}. Half of the spans are written after {{half_practice}} teacher chains instead of {{half_watch3}}, {{saving}} times fewer.
  • The levels arrive in order. Spans of 2, 4 and 8 links are half written after {{stair12_1}}, {{stair12_2}} and {{stair12_3}} episodes. No schedule is set: a join for table \(k\) needs units from table \(k/2\).
  • Reading grows with writing. An entry is also a unit that the learner can check in one look-up. So the spans it reads as one unit are the spans it writes (circles on the lines). The links it accepts do not change with practice.
  • Practice cannot supply a pair the teacher has not shown. After 512 teacher chains the learner accepts {{stair_q9}} of the links, and practice stops at {{n9_p1}}, {{n9_p2}} and {{n9_p3}} of the spans of 2, 4 and 8 links.
  • It needs watching first. The same examples give {{sched_watch_first}} of the spans of 8 links if the learner watches and then practises, and {{sched_practise_first}} in the opposite order.
  • It does not beat fresh teacher chains. Of \(2^{21}\) tokens, all spent on teacher chains give {{split21_all}} of the spans of 8 links, and half spent on practice give {{split21_half}}. A teacher chain of {{n}} states holds 13 spans, and a join holds one.

4. A generator that starts at random: only its own samples prune it

A generator has to start somewhere. Here every table starts with {{tandem_rows}} random entries of count 1. Almost all of them are wrong: \(c\) does not lie on a route from \(a\) to \(b\). The teacher shows 4,096 chains, which complete the pair table. Three learners then spend the same number of tokens.

  • Practice with the signed rule. Half joins, half rehearsals, as defined in section 1.
  • Practice, failures ignored. The same, but a failed test leaves the count as it is.
  • Teacher chains. The learner only watches.
  • The signed rule removes the random entries. A rehearsed random entry fails the check and loses its count. {{tandem_signed_left3_23}} of those in the top table are left at the end, and 99% of the learner's own chains are valid after {{tandem_signed_t99}} tokens.
  • Teacher chains never remove one. They do not come from the random entries, so they never test them. They add right entries, which makes the random ones a smaller share of what the learner draws. Own chains are {{tandem_teacher_own3_20}} valid after \(2^{20}\) tokens, and 99% valid only after {{tandem_teacher_t99}}.
  • Counting up is not enough. Without the minus all random entries stay, and {{tandem_plus_own3_20}} of own chains are valid after \(2^{20}\) tokens.
  • Teacher chains remain better at adding what is missing. At the end the watching learner writes {{tandem_teacher_write3_23}} of the population's spans of 8 links, and the practising one {{tandem_signed_write3_23}}.
  • A heavier random start widens the difference. With 65,536 random entries per table, {{tandem_big_signed_own3_23}} of own chains are valid at the end with the signed rule, {{tandem_big_teacher_own3_23}} with teacher chains and {{tandem_big_plus_own3_23}} without the minus.
  • Lower tables are pruned more slowly. {{tandem_signed_left1_23}} of the random entries of the bottom table are left at the end. The right entries there have large counts, so the random ones are rarely drawn.

What this rests on. The recogniser is complete after a few hundred chains, long before the generator (section 2), and it rejects whatever it has not seen. So it can judge the generator's samples from the start. The random entries are an assumption. A table has no bias of its own, and we put them in by hand. The same holds for wrong entries that come from the teacher: if the teacher slips on 3% of its states and the generator takes its chains as shown, {{judge_teacher_own3}} of own chains are valid, however many chains follow.

5. What the model shows, and what it does not

Shows

  • A recogniser and a generator trained on the same data by the same rule, in which the recogniser is ahead by a factor that grows with depth and follows a counting law.
  • Generation that improves offline, by writing and judging, towards an exact target. In a world with many routes the practised tables come within {{bridge_cond2}} of the exact ones in total variation.
  • Deeper levels that arrive later without a schedule.
  • One signed, local rule that builds the generator and prunes it.

Does not show

  • Practice that beats fresh teacher chains. We found two exceptions, each with an added assumption. In a world of 16 regions, where most teacher chains lie outside what the learner reads, practice writes {{reach_own_home3_23}} of the home region's spans of 8 links and teacher chains {{reach_fresh_home3_23}}. And if the learner may skip, at no cost, the joins it already holds, nine in ten spans take {{aim_u_aim_t90}} tokens instead of {{aim_u_fresh_t90}}.
  • Where spurious entries come from. They are put in by hand.
  • More teacher chains that make the learner worse. This happens only with a teacher who slips.
  • A hidden cause. The recogniser judges correctness and infers no latent variable.
  • Generalisation. Tables do not generalise across states, and writing is hard only in one pass: a pair table applied link by link already writes valid chains.
  • A mapping to in-context learning.
