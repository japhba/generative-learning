Can a generator teach its recognizer and learn faster?

A controlled six-bit Helmholtz-style model with a linear Gaussian decoder, a single affine recognition layer, and exact latent enumeration. All runs used local CPU; no paid services or external correctness checker.

With strongly overlapping causes, current-model dreams give 18.3× lower marginal error than a stable ELBO baseline at the same estimated compute allowance (95% interval 16.1–21.0; 12/12 seeds; fresh evaluation). Identical recognition rates also show a benefit. Orthogonal causes do not. This establishes a conditional finite-budget learning improvement; distinct asymptotic exponents remain unestablished.

Robustness: a stable baseline and identical learning rates

The initial tuned ELBO curve eventually worsens, so its 24.1× endpoint gap is not the most conservative comparison. A slower ELBO recognizer continues to improve. Against this stable baseline, current sleep achieves 18.3× lower error (95% interval 16.1–21.0; 12/12 seeds), verified with 16,384 fresh evaluation observations per seed after deterministic replay to the primary budget. Both generator rates are 0.01; recognition rates are 1 for the stable baseline and 10 for sleep.

Using the same recognition rate of 3 for both methods still gives 8.2× lower error with sleep on fresh evaluation (95% interval 5.6–10.8). At rate 1 the benefit is small. No shared rate shows a clear gain for orthogonal causes. This supports a benefit from the recognition objective and its useful learning-rate range, rather than an unconditional advantage of generating extra samples. These controls were added after the main confirmation and are labeled as follow-up analyses.

The measured CPU-time comparison also favors sleep: stable-baseline error / sleep error is 14.9 at a common training CPU allowance. This is implementation-specific timing, not a hardware-independent complexity law.

Mechanistic limit: sleep improves posterior coverage and weight recovery, but the instantaneous squared generator-gradient bias is not uniformly smaller. At the primary budget it is 0.006645 with sleep versus 0.006223 for the initially selected ELBO baseline. The results do not establish that a smaller gradient-bias norm is the causal explanation.

What mechanism is being tested?

Each observation combines six independent binary causes. Their decoder columns have unit norm and equal pairwise overlap. Overlap introduces explaining-away interactions in the posterior:

log p(h | x) = xᵀWh / sigma² − hᵀWᵀWh / (2 sigma²) + constant(x)
generator gradient = [W E_data E_q(hhᵀ | x) − E_data x E_q(h | x)ᵀ] / sigma²

Initially q is uniform, so the data–latent cross moment vanishes and generator learning shrinks W. Sleep supplies internally generated causes together with observations, teaching q an inverse mapping. The generator itself is updated only from real observations. We measure whether this makes its approximate update closer to exact likelihood learning.

Frozen dreams use the same initial model throughout. At overlap 0.8 their error divided by current-dream error is 74.52. Exact posterior teaching uses real observations and the current model's enumerated posterior, without true latent labels or dreams; sleep error divided by this control is 1.31. This control distinguishes an inference-training objective from synthetic-data generation itself. Exact likelihood bypasses the learned recognizer altogether.

Methods and limits

Teacher: h in {−1,+1}⁶ with independent fair bits; x = Wh + Normal(0, 0.7²I) in eight observed dimensions. WᵀW = (1−rho)I + rho11ᵀ. Initial W is 0.8 of the teacher plus weight noise of standard deviation 0.15/sqrt(8), deliberately giving the generator useful prior structure. Recognition starts at zero. This is a consolidation experiment with a warm generator, not learning without any information.

The primary recognizer is a 64-state categorical softmax of a single affine layer. It can represent the exact posterior; an unrestricted oracle can directly compile its weights from W. We compare specified learning rules, not all possible algorithms. The factorized control uses six logistic outputs. All latent expectations in wake updates are enumerated exactly, so the main comparison does not rely on a weak Monte Carlo variational-gradient estimator.

Independent Gaussian observations are sampled online in batches of 128. Twelve confirmation seeds (9300–9311) are disjoint from tuning seeds (9202, 9203). Each algorithm received the same tuning grid; settings were selected by log-error averaged over equal log-compute points from 512 to 8192. The selected rates are constant across overlaps. Both rates decay as 1/sqrt(1 + compute/1024 wake updates). Frozen sleep inherits current-sleep settings. Maximum allowance: 16,384 wake updates.

Marginal KL uses E_ptrue[exp(d)−1−d], d=log p_model−log p_true; this nonnegative estimator equals KL in expectation because both densities normalize. The ordinary excess NLL and test standard errors are also retained. Both marginal and recognition loss slopes are checked regularly; stationary runs stop after at least 2,048 updates. The result is a policy with a maximum compute allowance, not a claim that stopped models executed extra updates.

The controls and data do not show that unrestricted sampling and fitting from the same distribution creates information. A generator and its recognizer represent different conditional mappings. A successful sleep update transfers existing joint-model structure into the recognizer while real observations continue to anchor generator learning.
