Follow-ups to the cross-task search · updated 6 October 2026

Does a model learn better from its own laid-out reasoning?

Native traces · Laid-out reasoning · Correctness-matched · Further search · Small students · Edge of capability · Language · Recognition vs application · Procedures · Cross-task search

No robust own-source advantage anywhere. Once own and foreign practice are matched (same questions, process-correct, similar length), no model learns more from its own text — not from polished worked solutions (pre-registered confirmation: Gemma +1.1 pp [−4.4, +7.5]), not from its private thinking traces (first cohort +3.2 pp, pre-registered replication +0.7 pp [−1.1, +2.5]), and not when the same solutions are used as fine-tuning data (−0.4 pp [−4.6, +3.7]). Every positive exploratory cohort was singled out by Gemma 4 and failed fresh confirmation. Own text is measurably more familiar (fine-tuning loss 0.10–0.15 vs 0.3–1.1 per token), but familiarity did not turn into better learning. When readers may reason at test and practice is unmatched, own traces help least. Update (6 Oct): at the edge of capability with the number correct matched, own attempts help less than peers' when they include mistakes (§8); the one replicated own advantage is small and in fine-tuning of small students (+1.5 pp, §7). Update (7 Oct): comprehensibility matters at the level of language: a same-language peer's worked solution helps more than an other-language one (+4.1 pp, pre-registered), but the reader's own solutions help less than a same-language peer's (§10). Holding recognition fixed removes the language effect: other-language solutions are understood less, not applied worse (§11). But for deploying a demonstrated multi-step procedure, models do better from their own worked examples than from a same-language peer's (+5.3 pp, pre-registered), without recognising them better (§12).

1 · Native traces: free-form worked solutions, readers also reason at test

Seven models (two Qwen, two Llama, Gemma 4, Phi-4, Mistral-Nemo) each wrote free-form worked solutions to 8 practice questions with no teacher examples, so every trace is in the model's own idiom. Every reader then solved 12 test questions per episode, reasoning step by step, after 8 traces from one source (7 sources, same questions). 3 tasks chosen by baseline only (calendar arithmetic, number sequences, time intervals) × 6 episodes.

2 · Laid-out reasoning: hidden rules, answer-only tests

Your hypothesis: learning needs one's reasoning laid out in front of oneself. The native-traces design could not test it, because readers reasoned again at test time. Here every episode has a hidden rule shown by 4 examples (mystery operator a◇b = p·a+q·b+r; Caesar cipher; alien unit conversions with two hidden ratios). Six models reason out loud on 8 practice problems. At test, readers must answer directly, no working, after one source's practice either in full or reduced to its final answers. 18 episodes × 12 tests.

Example: Gemma's own operator trace (episode 5, rule 3a+3b−6), complete text
To find the hidden rule, let's examine the relationship between the two numbers and the result in each example.

**Example 1: 59 ★ 24 = 243**
Let's look at the digits:
$5 \times 9 = 45$
$2 \times 4 = 8$
This doesn't seem to lead to 243 directly.
Let's try multiplying the first number by something and the second by something.
Or, let's try multiplying the digits of the first number and the second number in a different way.
Let's look at the sum of the digits:
$5+9=14$; $2+4=6$.
Let's try: (First number $\times$ second digit of first number) + (Second number $\times$ first digit of second number)? No.

Let's look at the products of the digits:
Example 1: $(5 \times 9) + (2 \times 4) = 45 + 8 = 53$. (No)
Example 1: $(5+9) \times (2+4) = 14 \times 6 = 84$. (No)

Let's try a different approach: **Multiply the digits of the first number, then multiply the digits of the second number, and combine them?** No.
Let's try: **(First number $\times$ 4) + (Second number $\times$ something)?**
$59 \times 4 = 236$. $243 - 236 = 7$.
$19 \times 4 = 76$. $156 - 76 = 80$. (No)

Let's try: **(First digit $\times$ Second number) + (Second digit $\times$ Second number)?** No, that's just the first number.

Let's look at the numbers again:
1. $59, 24 \rightarrow 243$
2. $19, 35 \rightarrow 156$
3. $57, 47 \rightarrow 306$
4. $30, 33 \rightarrow 183$

Let's try: **(Sum of digits of first number) $\times$ (Sum of digits of second number)?**
1. $14 \times 6 = 84$
2. $10 \times 8 = 80$
3. $12 \times 11 = 132$
4. $3 \times 6 = 18$
Notice a pattern?
$84 \times 3 = 252$ (Close to 243)
$80 \times 2 = 160$ (Close to 156)
$132 \times 2 = 264$ (Close to 306)

Let's try: **(First number + Second number) $\times$ something?**
1. $59 + 24 = 83 \rightarrow 83 \times 3 = 249$. ($249 - 6 = 243$)
2. $19 + 35 = 54 \rightarrow 54 \times 3 = 162$. ($162 - 6 = 156$)
3. $57 + 47 = 104 \rightarrow 104 \times 3 = 312$. ($312 - 6 = 306$)
4. $30 + 33 = 63 \rightarrow 63 \times 3 = 189$. ($189 - 6 = 183$)

**The Hidden Rule is: (First Number + Second Number) $\times$ 3 - 6.**

Let's verify with all examples:
1. $(59 + 24) \times 3 - 6 = 83 \times 3 - 6 = 249 - 6 = 243$. (Correct)
2. $(19 + 35) \times 3 - 6 = 54 \times 3 - 6 = 162 - 6 = 156$. (Correct)
3. $(57 + 47) \times 3 - 6 = 104 \times 3 - 6 = 312 - 6 = 306$. (Correct)

3 · Correctness-matched: own vs foreign solutions of the same correctness

Every source's practice consists of correct worked solutions to the same questions (several samples per question; the first qualifying one per model is used; only questions every model solved are kept). The test reply is prefilled with “Answer:”, so no reader can reason or ignore the format. No model's text is rewritten. Models are served from pinned weights on gpu_lowp (vLLM 0.28).

5c · Reasoning allowed at test, matched practice

With the matched practice of 3c and step-by-step reasoning at test, every reader reaches 100% after a single correct solution from any source (N = 1 and N = 6): a ceiling, so no own-source effect can show. (An answer-extraction bug — “Final Answer:\n\nAnswer: 7” parsed as “Answer: 7” — first produced a spurious +18 pp for Qwen3-30B; fixed everywhere, it changed 140 of 4,860 scores here, 7 of 12,096 in the native-traces study and 125 of 53,248 practice checks.)

6 · What remained open after section 5

Two limits recurred: readers that cannot answer directly sit at a floor, and readers allowed to reason hit a ceiling. Sections 7–8 address this with small students (fine-tuning) and with tasks placed at the edge of each panel's capability.

7 · Small students, fine-tuned: the one own advantage that holds up

Four small students from four families (Qwen3-1.7B, Llama-3.2-3B, SmolLM3-3B, Phi-4-mini), LoRA-fine-tuned on GSM8K solutions with identical problems and correctness for every source, evaluated on 500 GSM8K test problems; two training seeds per run.

  • Own generated solutions vs peers' (1,718 problems): +1.3 [+0.2, +2.5] pp, pooled over seeds (seed 0 +1.5, seed 1 +1.1). Per student: Qwen3-1.7B +5.9, Llama-3.2-3B +1.0, SmolLM3-3B +3.2, Phi-4-mini -4.8. A large model's data (Qwen3-30B) is still better than own data (-1.5 pp own − large).
  • Same content, own wording (each model rewrote the same reference solutions in its own words, every step and number kept): own rewording vs peers' rewordings +1.5 [+0.2, +2.8] pp pooled over seeds, with the same per-student pattern (Qwen3-1.7B +7.1, Llama-3.2-3B -2.9, SmolLM3-3B +2.9, Phi-4-mini -1.2). Rewordings of any model beat the terse original human solutions by about 9 pp.
  • In context, the same solutions give no own advantage (-2.8 [-4.8, -0.8] pp). Re-scoring with the numeric scorer (last number on the answer line) leaves both fine-tuning effects unchanged (+1.6 pp each).

The effect is small and carried by two of the four students (Qwen3-1.7B, SmolLM3), with Phi-4-mini reversed in both studies, so it reads as "some models learn better from text in their own style" rather than a general law.

8 · At the edge of capability, matched in number correct

Hypothesis: a model learns more easily from its own attempts, right or wrong, when the task is at the edge of what it can do. Design: hidden-rule “alien units” problems; four similar small models each make 6 attempts per practice problem; a practice set shows 2 right + 2 wrong unchecked attempts with identical problems and right/wrong pattern for every source; the reader then solves new problems step by step. S = mean(own) − mean(peers) cancels “this source helps everyone”. 120 episodes, 65 with a matched mixed set.

  • Mixed practice: S = -6.2 [-8.4, -4.0] pp (pre-registered one-sided test for an own advantage p = 1.00). Without the much weaker Llama-3.2-3B: -5.3 [-7.9, -2.7] pp, every pair negative.
  • All-correct practice: -2.8 [-4.0, -1.5] pp; without Llama -0.1 [-1.2, +0.8] pp (a tight null).
  • Marking the wrong attempts as wrong changes nothing (+0.0 [-1.8, +1.9] pp). Practice itself helps: +12.6 [+9.7, +15.6] pp (mixed), +26.5 [+24.8, +28.3] pp (all correct).
  • A first run was void: exact-match scoring mis-read formats such as “Answer: **Answer: 30**”, so the right/wrong pattern was not really matched and readers that copied a format were marked wrong (protocol amendment 4).

Language matters, self does not. A solution written by a model of the reader's language helps it about 4–5 pp more than that source's general quality predicts, and the gap does not close with more examples. The reader's own solutions help less than a same-language peer's. Other-language solutions are recognised less often and, at N ≥ 2, applied worse even when the rule was recognised (+4–6 pp on the interaction scale; not at N = 1). The effect is carried by the English and Japanese readers; the Chinese readers show none.

12 · Recognising vs deploying a demonstrated procedure: the first own advantage

The earlier tasks could not separate recognition from deployment: “recognition” meant stating a rule (itself generative) and applying a known rule was trivial. Here writers are told a hidden 3–4-step number procedure (e.g. swap first and last digits → subtract 563 → sort digits ascending → multiply by 9) and write worked executions in their language; readers see only 1–4 worked examples and either recognise the steps (4-way questions, “which describes step 2?”) or deploy the procedure on new numbers. Each reader × source cell got 3 × L step questions plus 4 verification items (number chains, half with one wrong step); 39 fresh episodes were added. The deployment own effect replicates on the fresh episodes (+5.5 pp [+3.1, +7.8]). Verification is at chance for every reader, so it carries no information. On step identification, four readers recognise every step from their own and their peer's example (97–100%; own − peer 0.0 pp), yet deploy the procedure +7.6 pp [+5.2, +10.0] better from their own (OLMo +15, GLM +13, Phi-4-mini +6, InternLM −3; pre-registered before the fresh episodes were analysed). 13 · Where this leaves the idea


Reading one's own worked solutions in context gives no advantage when the hard part is working out a rule: with correct practice own and peer solutions are equal, with mistakes own attempts help less (§8), and language affects only how often the rule is recognised (§10–11). The one in-context own advantage appears when the hard part is executing a demonstrated multi-step procedure: models deploy it better from their own worked executions than from a same-language peer's, without recognising them better (§12, pre-registered). In the weights, small students fine-tuned on their own wording learn about 1.5 pp more (§7). Next: longer procedures and a second procedural family, and fine-tuning on own vs peer executions.
