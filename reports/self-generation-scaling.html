Self-generation improves sample efficiency

Batch r5s · Updated 2026-09-08T12:10:30.051173+01:00 · Three fresh training seeds, both methods, separate final evaluation

Refreshing the generator achieves lower held-out error with four times fewer candidate answers: 0.210% after 65,536 candidates, versus 0.573% after 262,144 for the frozen-source learner. This comparison uses directly evaluated checkpoints. Both students train only on verified correct examples, and both continue improving.

At the matched budget of 65,536 candidates, strict-test error is 0.210% for current-source training and 1.930% for frozen-source training: an error ratio of 9.20, with a 95% paired hierarchical interval of 7.41–11.46. Error ratios and sample-budget savings are different quantities.

This is feedback-guided self-generation. The model supplies its own candidate answers, and an external correctness check selects usable training examples. Question prompts are IID. The accepted input distribution changes as the generator improves. The result applies to this one-token counting task and model; it does not establish an advantage without validation or over direct uniform gold-label supervision.

A control that can continue learning

The previous s4g frozen control fitted some incorrect selected answers. Here both methods reject an entire two-candidate group if neither answer is correct. Otherwise they fit a uniformly selected correct candidate. Both students update all model weights. The generator uses either the student's current weights or an unchanged initial copy.

Accepted examples and compute accounting

The frozen initial generator rarely supplies correct examples from the initially difficult count-0 group. Refreshing brings that group's share of accepted examples toward its one-third share of the IID prompt distribution. Every accepted token was independently audited against its sampled candidate and correct answer; no correct target is supplied when both candidates fail.

Actual forward/backward work is matched by the harness. The second plot uses a counterfactual optimized-filter proxy: proposal tokens plus three times accepted training tokens. It allows a frozen learner to save the work of rejected rows. This is a token-work estimate, not a measured FLOP count. Matching accepted examples also changes optimizer update count, so that axis does not alone isolate intrinsic per-example efficiency.

At 1% error, the interpolated saving is 4.94 times on accepted examples and 5.34 times on the optimized-filter work proxy. These secondary estimates use mean curves without new confidence intervals. The candidate advantage therefore persists under both alternative accounting choices; a wall-clock speedup was not measured.

A normalized probabilistic toy predicts an improvement with the same exponent

A single layer of independent Bernoulli/Boltzmann modes has linear energies and logistic normalization. Let each mode's initial chance of supplying a correct two-candidate example be q0 = p0(2-p0). With equally frequent modes and accepted-mean updates, the derived asymptotic laws are:

current mean error ~ m / t
frozen mean error ~ mean(q0) × sum(1/q0) / t
candidate-budget gain = mean(q0) × mean(1/q0) ≥ 1

Both exponents are 1. Heterogeneous initial difficulty produces a better coefficient when the generator refreshes. With a single equally difficult mode, accepted-mean population updates coincide. The inequality therefore attributes the gain to heterogeneous usable practice, rather than merely multiplying the loss by a higher acceptance rate.

These gates are exactly conditional probabilities of an RBM with binary visible vector x, one binary hidden answer h, energy E(x,h) = −h w·x, and partition function Z = 2^m + product(1+exp(w_j)), evaluated on one-hot practice inputs. This supplies a normalized probabilistic model with explicit weights. The training objective is the verified conditional-answer objective; joint-density learning and contrastive divergence are separate questions.

For the fixed toy probabilities (0.2, 0.75, 0.95), the predicted gain is 1.236. Independent finite-batch simulations retain the predicted amplitudes within about 0.31% at their endpoints. Their finite-window slopes are close to 1, with finite-time deviations fully recorded. The toy is a mechanism demonstration and its modes are independent.

The toy also has an interpretable weight prediction. In population dynamics, refreshing removes the spread between mode logits as inverse time squared, whereas frozen sampling leaves a limiting spread set by the initial acceptance probabilities. Both weight directions eventually approach the equally positive direction, with different rates. These are overlaps with a balanced solution direction, not a finite true parameter. The unfitted formulas were derived and checked retrospectively on stored trajectories; finite-batch noise changes the faster population alignment tail.

A secondary hypothesis plugs the LLM's initial monitoring probabilities into this coefficient formula, giving 5.17, with an initial-question bootstrap interval 3.51–7.49. It was frozen at 2026-09-07T21:17:23.592590+01:00, after early confirmation checkpoints through 768 existed and before the 2048/4096 targets. It assumes independent equally weighted latent modes with a common gradient metric. Those assumptions are not established for the LLM; the interval excludes model misspecification, and this eventual prefactor is not an exact finite-budget forecast.

This coefficient formula is not quantitatively validated for the LLM. At the last strict-test checkpoint, the matched-budget error ratio is 13.16, whereas the toy-inspired eventual coefficient hypothesis is 5.17. The horizontal saving to a chosen error threshold is a different quantity outside a common inverse-budget asymptote. The established connection is the usable-practice mechanism and a scaling improvement, rather than agreement of all coefficients or exponents.

A shared skill can change the exponent

A second construction, developed after the LLM results, uses the same verified-only update but replaces independent mode weights with one shared trainable scalar. Problems have difficulty d ≥ 0 drawn IID with density a exp(−a d). The model's correct-answer probability is sigmoid(theta − d). Learning theta on easier questions also improves the chance of solving harder ones.

For 0 < a < 1, the population dynamics have analytically different exponents:

frozen generator: mean error ~ constant × t^(−a)
current generator: mean error ~ 2 / [a²(3−a) t]
shared weight: theta_frozen ~ log(t), theta_current ~ log(t)/a

Time is learning rate times optimizer updates, proportional to candidate budget at fixed batch size and learning rate. Frozen proposals keep supplying relatively easy examples whose remaining gradients shrink. Current proposals supply correct answers near the model's increasing difficulty threshold, sustaining useful practice. Total-acceptance normalization remains present in both arms.

For the illustrative choice a=1/2, the predicted exponents are 1 for current and 1/2 for frozen. Numerical integration gives 1.000000 and 0.500006 over population times 1e8–1e12, with derived amplitudes also recovered. This is integration of population equations, not a simulation of that many updates or a new LLM run. Greedy error has the same exponent separation in this regime, so the construction is not solely about sharpening confidence.

The model is a normalized conditional Boltzmann model with one linear logit. A binary/continuous joint embedding has energy a d + h(d−theta) and partition function 1/a + exp(theta)/(a+1). Practice inputs still follow the prescribed IID distribution. This does not require joint-density training or contrastive divergence.

The assumptions determine the outcome. If difficulty has a fixed upper bound, both sampled-error exponents eventually return to 1. They also agree at 1 when a > 1; the boundary a=1 has logarithmic corrections. All four checked settings, numerical-resolution checks, and an exact closed-form check at a=1/2 are retained. A broad difficulty range can therefore give a long interval of different slopes even when the final slopes agree.

This is a concrete theoretical mechanism for an exponent improvement under the same checker-guided sampling rule. It is not yet a quantitative theory of the LLM: a shared correctness direction and the relevant difficulty tail have not been established there. The earlier independent-mode toy remains valid under its own assumptions. See the complete derivation, assumptions and numerical checks.

The toy is a binary success model, not an implementation of counting. A direct compatibility check also rejects its literal one-skill description of the LLM: such a model would preserve every question's probability rank. At update 512, the initial-to-current rank correlation on 1023 already exposed monitoring questions is 0.466–0.506 across current-source seeds, and 0.848–0.862 across frozen-source students, instead of 1. The logit gains vary across questions, and rankings change within answer classes as well. All probability pairs have finite logits at this checkpoint. This is compatible with richer shared learning, but the scalar toy alone does not quantitatively describe the LLM.

Self-generation: scaling and probabilistic mechanisms

We derived and independently checked scaling laws in normalized probabilistic toys. Full-weight LLM self-training also improves substantially, but its learning curve changes regime and rejects the simplest RBM extrapolation. The shared update identity is exact; a shared constant exponent does not follow from it.

This study uses an external correctness checker. Both LLM conditions generate candidates and update their students. The comparison tests refreshing the generator against keeping its initial weights frozen; it does not isolate generation versus recognition, or establish improvement without feedback. The earlier ICL experiments are a separate setting and were not rerun here.

At a shared budget of 4,096 generated candidates (64 updates), current-source accuracy is 82.90%, compared with 69.43% for frozen-source training. The paired gain is 13.46 percentage points, with a 95% hierarchical interval of 12.11–14.87 points. This comparison uses identical budgets.

The three current-source runs reached a mean sampled accuracy of 99.744% at 2048 updates. Their finite-window exponents are about 1.46 over 128–1024 updates and1.71 over 256–2048 updates; local slopes change substantially, so these fits are not a stable shared toy/LLM law.

The shared mechanism

With current-policy sampling and a binary correctness checker, the expected score of the selected one of two trajectories equals the success gradient. Population gradient flow therefore satisfies dp/dt = ||grad p||². If squared gradient norm scales as excess error to power gamma, the corresponding error exponent is 1/(gamma−1), under the stated population assumptions.

The one-logit Boltzmann gate gives exponent 1. The existing Gaussian RBM gives exponent 1/2 because its continuous distribution of near-boundary margins changes the gradient geometry. Numerical population exponents are 1.000004 and 0.500049, respectively, over the predeclared late-time window. Their amplitudes were derived before integration.

The RBM's normalized weight overlap improves while its norm grows. These are sampled-correctness dynamics, not convergence of joint-density parameters to the true data-generating weights.

The weight-space connection, and where the simple reduction fails

For a common cubic relation, the training/test success-gradient overlap divided by error cubed should stabilize. Measuring this quantity directly tests the proposed RBM-to-LLM bridge more strongly than matching slopes by eye.

The LLM's displacement initially aligns with its initial held-out success gradient, but later learning changes direction. This is a measurable weight-space diagnostic. It is not an overlap with known true LLM weights, which we do not have; the RBM's overlap with its known generative parameter is a different observable. Gradient rotation can also occur in a fixed-feature classifier, so this observation alone does not prove that the model learned new features.

Answer groups expose a further failure of a homogeneous gate. In the pilot, correct-count 0 performance rose from 20.7% to 87.7% by 128 updates. Count1 performance was then about 79.0%, and improved sharply later. A single aggregate slope can conceal these successive changes.

Direct evidence from class-gradient overlaps

A final retrospective probe tests the suggested low-dimensional explanation. The gradients for count 0 and count 1 initially have cosine +0.295, but this changes to −0.195 by 32 updates and −0.588 by 128 updates. An update helping one class can therefore locally hurt the other, while the total update can still improve both through their own gradient terms. This is a concrete change in the measured coupling between learning modes.

Three fixed initial class-gradient directions capture only 42.1% of the pilot's final squared parameter displacement. A coupled three-gate model using those initial overlaps, initial probabilities and the actual learning rate predicts about 94.0% accuracy at 64 updates, versus 83.4% observed. Its late endpoint is closer, but it misses the intervening behavior. This reduction was designed after seeing the class curves and is explicitly retrospective; it is not presented as an independently validated forecast.

The exact class-level relation is d p_a / d t = sum_b P(b) <grad p_a, grad p_b> within the corresponding population or empirical observer distribution. This gives a principled route from overlap measurements to learning rates. The missing ingredient in the fixed toy reductions is the evolution of these overlaps, not evidence that the selected-score identity fails.
