Scaling to a million latent configurations

The new range is 16–1,048,576 configurations: 65,536-fold in problem size and 256 times the previous maximum. The same six prespecified seeds are tested at all nine sizes, with both learning rules and both Adam stabilizers: 216 training runs, plus 12 CPU-to-GPU bridge runs. Every run has finished; no live trajectories are included. Every point on the new curve uses the same float64 GPU implementation and evaluation schedule. Earlier CPU results remain below as historical comparisons.

The size-dependent cost advantage survives the numerical control. With ε=10⁻¹², full-range fitted powers are 1.48 for ELBO and 1.06 for ELBO + sleep. The seed-bootstrap intervals are [1.47, 1.52] and [1.06, 1.07]. The baseline’s exponent changes as the fitting window moves, so this supports a clear finite-range scaling advantage without establishing one fixed exponent for all sizes.

With ε=10⁻⁸, the million-state mean dense-compute ratio is 1,720.1× (median ratio 1,695.7×; paired-seed interval [1,526.1, 1,910.0]). With ε=10⁻¹², the million-state mean dense-compute ratio is 231.9× (median ratio 239.4×; paired-seed interval [197.7, 266.3]).

Over the new 4,096–1,048,576 range, both overlap-target cost curves are approximately linear: their fitted powers are 0.96 and 1.02. Reference FLOPs, validation and interpretation

The GPU audit covers three generative depths and three objectives. Gradients and 100-step weight trajectories agree with the NumPy implementation to numerical precision; restoring the complete optimizer tensors reproduces continuation exactly. Full checkpoints include recognition weights, Adam moments/counter, the constant learning-rate specification and RNG states. They are written locally and copied asynchronously to persistent storage. Evaluation and stationarity checks continue throughout long runs.

The bridge check exposed sensitivity of first-hit target cost to evaluation spacing: a brief error dip may be missed by a coarse schedule even when shared-checkpoint errors agree closely. For that reason all smaller sizes were rerun with the new schedule; the new large points were not simply appended to older coarse measurements. The raw trajectories and bridge discrepancies remain available.

This remains a fixed-generator population inference experiment. The two-layer sigmoid belief network is normalized, and its exact posterior is realizable by the affine recognizer. No verifier, rejection, reward or temperature adjustment enters either objective. The recognizer explicitly allocates one output to every latent configuration, and the posterior coefficients could also be compiled directly from the generator. The comparison therefore concerns specified learning rules and their recovery behavior; it is not an algorithm-independent computational lower bound, a finite-sample scaling law or a new LLM result. Across that 256-fold range in state count, descriptive fitted cost exponents are 1.64 for ELBO and 1.07 for ELBO + sleep. Paired-seed bootstrap intervals are [1.50, 1.78] and [1.04, 1.09]. At 4,096 states the cost ratio is 75.3× using means and 41.3× using medians; the mean ratio’s seed-bootstrap interval is [34.8, 133.3].

There is also an exact weight-space identity for the affine toy. If e is the difference from the compiled posterior weights, wake’s recognition gradient is M(B)e, where M is the current recognition Fisher matrix. The expected forward/sleep gradient is [∫ M(B* + s(B−B*)) ds]e: an average of Fisher matrices along the path to the posterior. This explains why neglected directions can receive weak wake updates while forward fitting still moves them. Both operators agree locally near the correct posterior. We checked posterior compilation, both gradient identities and the weight-distance contraction across 36 cases, with maximum error below 1.5×10⁻¹⁵. This is an affine-toy identity, not a global identity or exponent theorem for the nonlinear LLM. Derivation · Audit results.

Earlier shorter-budget results, for comparison

The more reproducible toy result

A two-layer directed sigmoid belief network generates six binary observations from hidden binary causes. For this size experiment, the useful generator is fixed and exactly matches the data distribution; only recognition weights learn. The recognizer is an affine softmax over complete latent configurations. At moderate initial recognition bias, 16 new initialization seeds give finite-range fits C(K) ∝ K^1.66 for ELBO and C(K) ∝ K^1.07 for ELBO plus sleep, where K ranges from 16 to 1,024 latent configurations and the target is posterior KL ≤ 0.1. Seed-bootstrap intervals are [1.53, 1.82] and [1.03, 1.10]; they do not cover model or extrapolation uncertainty.

The measured mean compute ratio grows from 2.1× at 16 configurations to 29.8× at 1,024. These are actual compute-to-target ratios, unlike error ratios in the previous Gaussian experiment. Every replication run reached the primary target. A further 64-run audit retuned each method separately at every size, using an equal rate grid and independent tuning seeds; it selected the same rates at all four sizes, so no confirmation cells needed rerunning. The baseline’s adjacent-size slopes vary substantially, so the fitted exponent is a finite-range description.

The initial three-seed fit suggested exponents 2.02 and 1.15. Before running 4,096 configurations, we saved its extrapolation. Sleep required 2.37 billion estimated FLOPs versus a forecast of 2.36 billion, agreeing within checkpoint resolution. ELBO exceeded its point forecast: two runs reached the target and one remained above it after its budget, giving a mean-cost lower bound of 744 billion FLOPs versus a forecast of 472 billion. We therefore have no observed finite mean target cost for that ELBO check, and do not fit the censored limit as if it were a success.

What the deeper experiments establish

The broader sweep includes one- and three-layer generative models, one- and three-layer recognizers, SGD and Adam, population and sampled gradients, and fixed and coupled generators. There are 1,296 main runs, 324 rate follow-ups, 48 initial size runs, 96 size controls, 128 independent size replications and six extrapolation runs, plus 376 pilot/tuning runs. Rates were searched on separate seeds, including checks beyond the original grid boundaries.

Random recognition logit biases deliberately create unequal confidence: standard deviations 0, 3 and 6. This is an explicit controlled initialization, not a claim that standard pretraining naturally produces these exact biases. Strong neglect gives large recovery improvements, but sampled sleep can leave a noise floor and the fitted error-versus-compute slopes vary or reverse later. Adding depth alone did not establish a new asymptotic exponent.

Population and coupled-generator curves

An actual LLM match

We trained Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct using two separate rank-four LoRA adapters on its query/value projections. The pretrained backbone is frozen. One adapter predicts an output digit given an input and a named arithmetic rule; the other infers a rule from two input–output examples. The latent prior is uniform over eight rules. There are 480 training observations and 240 held-out observations, with disjoint ordered input pairs.

Wake updates use unlabeled real observations: the hidden rule identity is removed before training. ELBO expectations enumerate all eight rules, so the baseline does not rely on a poor discrete-gradient estimator. In sleep, the model samples a rule and its own output digits, then learns to recover that rule from the imagined examples. All generated examples are retained, including arithmetically incorrect ones. Temperature is one; no correctness checker, reward, or true rule label enters the updates. Both directions train in the final comparison.

The conditional output supports are explicitly restricted to ten digit tokens and eight letter tokens, identically across arms. This makes a tractable probabilistic model inside an LLM; it is not unrestricted chain-of-thought self-training. The generator’s initial exact posterior identifies a compatible rule on about 82% of the held-out examples, while direct recognition is initially at 12.5%.

The LLM’s finite-window error exponents are approximately 0.61 versus 0.88 over 64–256 baseline-update-equivalent compute, but 0.41 versus 0.46 over 128–512. This is evidence of a learning-curve advantage, not a stable exponent separation. Generator marginal likelihood does not show a comparable sleep advantage; the demonstrated improvement is inference.

Mechanism and the next discriminating test

A generated cause–observation pair provides the inverse model with a cause label it may rarely propose itself. Forward posterior fitting penalizes missing such explanations. Near a correctly learned posterior, however, both KL directions have the same Fisher curvature, even through a deep recognition network. Any stronger asymptotic scaling claim must therefore involve persistent recovery of increasingly difficult directions, changing representations, or a nonlocal regime—not depth by itself.

The main remaining limitation is structural: the toy explicitly enumerates latent configurations and gives the recognizer one output per configuration. Its cost is exponential in the number of latent bits. Its affine posterior can also be compiled directly from the generative weights, so this is a comparison between specified learning rules, not an algorithm-independent advantage. A stronger follow-up should use an autoregressive recognizer over compositional causes, grow the same hypothesis family in the LLM, and test whether the advantage survives equal-compute posterior approximations. The present eight-rule LLM experiment establishes the mechanism’s practical match, not that larger hypothesis spaces reproduce the toy’s numerical exponents.
